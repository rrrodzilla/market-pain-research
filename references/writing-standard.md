# Writing standard

Write the deliverable so its readers can understand, decide and act with the least effort. This standard condenses a strict, reader-centred approach to professional writing (it follows Jean-luc Doumont's principles of effective communication) and applies it to this skill's deliverable. It is self-contained: no other skill is needed. Do not mention the standard or its source in the deliverable.

## 1. Three priorities, in order

1. **Adapt to the reader.** Choose content, detail, order and words for the people who must decide. The writer does the work so the reader does not have to.
2. **Maximize signal over noise.** Keep what helps the reader reach the purpose; cut or move what costs attention without helping.
3. **Repeat usefully.** Restate the important messages where they help orientation: the summary, section leads, the chart title. Each repetition must serve the reader and must agree with the others.

At sentence level, prefer clarity, then accuracy, then brevity. Never shorten a claim by changing its meaning, dropping a needed qualifier, or hiding who did what.

## 2. Plan before drafting

Answer five questions, briefly, in `synthesis.md` (not in the deliverable):

| Question | Decision for this deliverable |
|---|---|
| Why | What should the reader be able to decide after reading? Usually: which pains to treat as established, which to treat as unvalidated, and whether to fund interviews. |
| Who | Who decides, what they already know about the market, and what they need explained. Assume subject expertise but no knowledge of this run. |
| What | The main message as one sentence, the findings that support it, and the detail needed to trust them. |
| When | When they will read it (often in a short slot before a meeting): the summary must stand alone. |
| Where | The medium (a shared doc, a page, Markdown) and how they will read it (on screen, possibly skimming). |

Write the main message as a full sentence that says what to conclude, not a topic. "Market pains" is a topic. "Four pains are well supported; seven others lean on vendor voices and need validation" is a message.

Make assumptions that matter visible to the user in chat, not in the prose. Never invent evidence, decisions or deadlines to fill a gap.

## 3. Structure

- **Global view first.** The title, the purpose, and the main message come first, so a reader who stops after the summary still leaves with the right conclusion. The executive summary plus decisions fit on about one page.
- **Work backward from conclusions.** Order sections by the reader's reasoning (decision, evidence, detail, next steps), not by the order the work was done.
- **Separate kinds of statement.** Keep observations (what findings say), interpretations (what the pattern means), recommendations (what to do) and open questions distinguishable. Keep each claim proportional to its evidence; narrow a claim the evidence does not fully support.
- **A manageable tree.** At most three heading levels; about five branches under any one node. Regroup if a section grows beyond that.
- **Orient before subdividing.** Open each section with a sentence that states its message before the table or bullets.
- **Detail where the interested reader can find it.** Methods and full evidence go to later sections and the index tab. Qualifications that change the decision stay next to the claims they govern.

## 4. Paragraphs and sentences

- **One purpose per paragraph.** State its message early, then support it with evidence, explanation or consequence.
- **Connect sentences.** Keep a stable subject across related sentences, or start the next sentence with the idea the previous one introduced. Use consistent terms: once a group is called "vendor-heavy", do not switch to "vendor-led" or "biased".
- **Main idea in the main clause.** Keep subjects near their verbs. Avoid long interruptions and nested clauses.
- **Familiar, precise words.** Keep the market's technical terms when they add precision; explain them only if the reader needs it.
- **Name the actor** when responsibility or judgment matters ("one practitioner reports", "vendors supply 47%"). Active voice by default.
- **Cut padding:** throat-clearing, announcements of what the document will do, unsupported adjectives, and contrasts with nothing behind them. Keep meaningful limits and uncertainty.
- **Keep scope in shortened claims:** who, how many, which period, and how strong the evidence is.
- **Check every calculation.**

## 5. Lists, tables and charts

- Use lists for parallel items or real sequences. Introduce each list with its purpose. Keep items grammatically parallel: all complete sentences, or all fragments that complete the lead-in.
- Do not break an argument into disconnected fragments; an argument belongs in a paragraph.
- Use tables for comparisons across the same attributes (themes, numbers, decisions).
- Use a chart for a relationship the reader decides from. Its title states the finding with its number; it uses one quiet colour plus one accent; it carries its numbers once, and the prose after it adds only what the picture cannot show.
- Captions, prose and the visual must agree.

## 6. Revision pass and acceptance criteria

Revise as a separate step after drafting. Check purpose and fidelity first, then structure, paragraphs, sentences and presentation. For each criterion record **pass**, **revise** or **unverifiable**, with the evidence. Fix every "revise", then recheck. A strength elsewhere does not cancel a failure.

| Criterion | Passes when |
|---|---|
| Purpose and audience | The deliverable enables the decision the user needs; vocabulary and detail fit the reader. |
| Main message and motivation | A reader grasps why it matters and what to conclude from the summary alone. |
| Support and fidelity | Each claim is supported to the strength stated; numbers and qualifiers match the `build/` files and findings; inferences are marked as such. |
| Hierarchy and navigation | Sections develop the summary; order follows the reader's reasoning; a reader can jump to a section and understand it. |
| Paragraph coherence | Every paragraph has one job, states it early, and has no unexplained topic shifts. |
| Sentence clarity and accuracy | Main clauses carry the ideas; actors and referents are clear; wording preserves meaning. |
| Signal and redundancy | Every element earns its place; repeated messages serve a purpose and agree everywhere. |
| Medium and mechanics | Length, layout, list grammar, labels and house style (no em dashes) comply. |

Three practical tests:

1. **Leads only.** Read only the headings and first sentences. The argument must still make sense.
2. **Deletion.** For each suspect sentence, imagine deleting it. If nothing is lost, delete it.
3. **Source comparison.** Compare each revised claim with its source to catch claims that grew stronger or weaker in revision.

Never report a check as passed that could not be performed, such as a chart you could not see rendered.

## 7. Common failures in this deliverable

| Failure | Fix |
|---|---|
| A summary bullet names themes by name while the decisions table uses numbers | Use "Themes 1-4" and the names together, everywhere |
| A sentence refers to something the reader has not seen ("in the key words recorded…") | Say it directly: "Across the findings, speakers say manual 38 times…" |
| A label narrower than its data ("Lowers risk" for findings about stress, fear and frustration) | Use the broader accurate label ("Relieves pain") |
| A phrase that presumes an outcome the user has not chosen ("before drafting value propositions") | Make it conditional ("If a focus is chosen, …") |
| One paragraph carrying two messages (a limit and a risk) | Split it, and move each to its own section |
| A product or company frame the user never stated | Remove it; ask the user if a comparison would help |
| Method or book names in the prose | Describe what was done in plain words |
